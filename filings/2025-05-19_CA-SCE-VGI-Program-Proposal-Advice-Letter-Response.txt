I. INTRODUCTION.
VGIC is a 501(c)6 membership-based advocacy group committed to advancing the role of
electric vehicles (“EV”) and vehicle-grid integration (“VGI”) through policy development,
education, outreach, and research. VGIC supports the transition to a decarbonized transportation
and electric sector by ensuring the value from EV deployments and flexible EV charging and
discharging is recognized and compensated to achieve a more reliable, affordable, and efficient
electric grid.
II. DISCUSSION.
VGIC commends SCE for proposing the Orchestrated Charging and Advanced Resiliency
for Distribution (“ORCHARD”) Program and for pursuing Low Carbon Fuel Standard (“LCFS”)
funding to support vehicle-grid integration (“VGI”).1 The ORCHARD Program would apply VGI
strategies to manage secondary local grid constraints, a growing concern as electrification increases
strain on the distribution system that current time-of-use rates alone cannot sufficiently alleviate.

By deploying VGI tools in ORCHARD, SCE can optimize the use of distribution assets, improve
cost-effectiveness, and, if implemented correctly, defer or avoid costly infrastructure upgrades.
Notably, SCE’s ORCHARD program design appears to directly respond to the California
Air Resources Board’s (“CARB”) updated LCFS regulation, which explicitly details the use of
LCFS holdback credit proceeds for VGI programs.2 CARB provides the following examples of uses
for these holdback credit proceeds:
i. Investments in grid-side distribution infrastructure necessary for EV charging.
ii. Support for vehicle-grid integration with projects such as:
I). Encouraging the optimization of EV charging through education in the following areas:
peak demand, rate pricing, grid emergencies, potential power shutoffs, infrastructure
deferral, renewable integration, and/or other signals and grid needs to provide grid and
customer benefits.
II). Providing program incentives to encourage driver participation in monitored/managed
charging, demand response, or vehicle-to-load / vehicle-to-grid applications.
III). Supporting the deployment and installation of bidirectional charging equipment.
IV). Other innovative approaches to promoting and managing EV charging and
discharging that provides benefits to customers and the grid.
iii. Hardware and software that decrease the cost of or avoid updates to infrastructure, including
load management software or outlet splitting.
The Commission and utilities should seize this no-regrets opportunity to launch non-
ratepayer-funded programs that deliver ratepayer benefits and generate critical data to inform the
broader VGI market.
While VGIC offers recommendations for the implementation of ORCHARD, we do not
believe that major Advice Letter modifications are required to address them. As such, VGIC strongly
supports the timely approval of the implementation plan as filed, and encourages SCE to remain
sufficiently flexible and responsive to customer and industry feedback during the implementation
period.
A. VGIC Supports Approval of ORCHARD at Full Scale, Including Bidirectional Charging
Incentives.
SCE proposes to use $23 million in LCFS Holdback Funding to create ORCHARD and has
an enrollment target of at least 25,000 customers. VGIC strongly supports this scale of deployment,
which represents a meaningful step forward for VGI and provides an opportunity to demonstrate
and maximize the cost-effectiveness of primarily software-based managed charging programs. As
discussed by SCE, achieving this scale for a VGI program will “demonstrate that these technically-
viable solutions generate significant return on investments.”3 Enrolling tens of thousands of

customers in these programs is critical, as “the benefits that VGI can provide to the grid start to
become material once significant numbers of customers are participating.”4 The costs associated
with scaling these largely software-based programs are relatively low, and having large amounts of
participation will maximize the cost-effectiveness of the investments SCE will make to create the
program.
VGIC also strongly supports SCE’s plan to offer incentives for bidirectional charging,
targeting 8,700 ORCHARD participants. Enabling bidirectional charging expands the grid services
that can be provided by EVs and advances multiple state goals, including:
• Reducing costs to ratepayers, including both EV drivers and non-EV customers,
• Advancing the state’s transportation electrification goals by reducing the total cost of EV
ownership,
• Supporting grid reliability, including during extreme heat events or other times of high stress
on the grid,
• Supporting customer and community resiliency, including during Public Safety Power
Shutoffs (“PSPS”) and other resilience events, and
• Fostering a growing market for VGI services, including job growth in manufacturing, field
technicians, and related employment opportunities.
ORCHARD participants installing bidirectional equipment will be able to access an $800
incentive to cover interconnection application costs. Offering an $800 incentive to offset
interconnection costs is critical to enabling bidirectional adoption, given that these fees greatly
exceed the standard $94 fee for similarly-sized rooftop solar customers (i.e., Net Energy Metering
or Net Billing Tariff customers) – which may even use the same inverter model as bidirectional
charging customers – and are a disproportionate burden for bidirectional charging systems. Unlike
stationary storage, the EV battery providing bidirectional energy has already been purchased for
another purpose, making interconnection costs one of the highest barriers to adoption (as opposed
to the purchase price of the energy storage system). Given the current high interconnection costs
and limited compensation mechanisms for EV battery discharge, many customers installing
bidirectional equipment currently are doing so only for backup power. For clarity, VGIC notes that
these backup-only system configurations do not currently require an $800 interconnection fee and,
instead, follow SCE’s longstanding safety rules applicable to the installation of customer-owned
backup generators (i.e., SCE requests the customer notify the utility of the system’s existence and
location, but the backup power system’s impact on the distribution grid does not need to go through
an interconnection study process).

To achieve SCE’s goal of enrolling 8,700 customers using grid-parallel bidirectional
charging systems (as opposed to islanded, backup-only systems), customers must be able to access
compensation mechanisms for grid-parallel EV battery discharge. Currently, SCE customers can
receive compensation for EV exports during grid emergencies if eligible to participate in the
Emergency Load Reduction Program (“ELRP”) or Demand Side Grid Support (“DSGS”) program.
However, these programs cannot be relied on to continue in the long term5 and have funding that
appears to be at risk.6 While customer export compensation that could apply to EV drivers was
envisioned in Energy Division staff’s CalFUSE dynamic pricing framework, SCE has elected not
to offer export compensation to EV customers in its new Expanded Dynamic Rate Pilot.7 Lastly,
SCE highlights its proposed Vehicle Grid Resource Proposal (“VGRP”) as a potential EV export
rate. VGIC encourages SCE and the Commission to move forward with establishing a static export
compensation rate for EVs that recognizes the full value provided by customer exports, for example,
as outlined in the Commission’s adopted Avoided Cost Calculator.
If there is no compelling revenue stream for EV exports at the time of implementation, SCE
should consider increasing the bidirectional charging customer incentive to achieve enrollment
goals. VGIC supports SCE’s plan to offer up to $7,800 in additional incentives for income-qualified
customers to help offset other equipment costs and ensure equitable access to bidirectional charging.
Similar equipment cost offsets could be offered to general market customers, or other incentive
structures could be explored. SCE should remain flexible and responsive to customer needs to
increase incentive levels for bidirectional charging systems, if needed.
B. VGIC supports SCE’s consideration that commercial fleets and medium- and heavy-duty
vehicles should also be eligible to receive the bidirectional charging system incentive.
While SCE’s proposal emphasizes residential enrollment, VGIC notes that SCE’s materials
reference both residential and commercial incentives as potentially eligible for bidirectional
charging incentives. The Advice Letter outlines goals of enrolling residential customers and
highlights the program’s focus on mitigating distribution congestion arising from light-duty EV
adoption.8 At the same time, the program documents note that bidirectional equipment incentives
will be “available to both residential and commercial customers.”
VGIC supports SCE’s vision on how to incorporate commercial and medium- and heavy-
duty (MDHD) into ORCHARD, specifically as it relates to bidirectional charging system incentives.

As SCE develops outreach and engagement strategies, including marketing and enrollment

C. VGIC believes SCE should be provided ample flexibility in contracting with one or more
aggregators in ORCHARD.
SCE indicates it will contract with at least one third-party aggregator and software provider
to implement ORCHARD. VGIC encourages SCE to engage with the party or parties that are best
suited to support customer outreach, program resilience, and interoperability concerns as revealed
through common competitive procurement practices. In the Advice Letter, SCE outlines potential
performance risks for ORCHARD, including “Aggregator software performance issues, such as a
lack of interoperability or falling short in performance, will affect the reliability and efficacy of
orchestrated charging.”9 By following long-established practices for developing partnership(s)
with industry leaders, SCE can directly address this risk.
