COMMENTS OF THE VEHICLE-GRID INTEGRATION COUNCIL ON ORDER
INSTITUTING RULEMAKING TO UPDATE DISTRIBUTION LEVEL
INTERCONNECTION RULES AND REGULATIONS
In accordance with the Rules of Practice and Procedure of the California Public Utilities
Commission (“Commission”), the Vehicle-Grid Integration Council (“VGIC”) hereby submits
these comments on the Order Instituting Rulemaking to Update Distribution Level Interconnection
Rules and Regulations (“OIR”), issued on August 20, 2025.
I. INTRODUCTION.
VGIC appreciates the opportunity to provide comments on the OIR to Update Rule 21.
VGIC strongly supports the Commission’s intent to modernize Rule 21 and ensure that
interconnection processes remain efficient, transparent, affordable, and equitable as California
transitions toward the use of more distributed energy resources (“DER”). In particular, VGIC urges
the Commission to prioritize reforms that enable bidirectional electric vehicle (“EV”) charging
customers to interconnect safely, affordably, and in a manner that supports grid reliability.
Accordingly, VGIC offers recommendations on the proposed scope, process, and schedule for this
proceeding.
II. BACKGROUND & INTEREST IN PROCEEDING.
VGIC is a 501(c)6 member-based advocacy group committed to advancing the role of EVs
and vehicle-grid integration (“VGI”) through policy development, education, outreach, and
1

research. VGIC supports the transition to a decarbonized transportation and electric sector by
ensuring the value from flexible EV charging and discharging is recognized and compensated to
achieve a more reliable, affordable, and efficient electric grid. VGIC’s members include
automotive original equipment manufacturers (“OEMs”), EV service providers (“EVSPs”), DER
aggregators, EV charging site developers, publicly owned electric utilities, and Community Choice
Aggregators.
In D.20-09-035, the Commission clarified that direct current (“DC”) bidirectional EV
charging systems are a form of energy storage for the purposes of interconnecting under Rule 21.
The Commission has also directed the investor-owned utilities (“IOUs”) to establish pathways to
interconnect alternating current (“AC”) bidirectional charging systems outside of the Rule 21
process. Lastly, the Commission has detailed next steps for incorporating AC bidirectional
charging systems into Rule 21, pending certain milestones completed by standards development
organizations. VGIC, therefore, holds a direct interest in modifications to Rule 21 interconnection
processes. VGIC has been an active party in the predecessor rulemaking on Rule 21, R.17-07-007,
and related rulemakings on transportation electrification and distributed energy resources, such as
R.18-12-006, R.21-06-017, and R.23-12-008.
III. COMMENTS ON THE PROPOSED SCOPE.
VGIC appreciates the Commission’s effort to consider the multitude of items that can be
improved in Rule 21. Below, VGIC offers the following recommendations for additional items
that should be included or excluded from the scope of this proceeding.
2

A. ITEMS TO ADD TO SCOPE.
a. Reassess Application Fees for Small Non-NEM and Non-NBT Systems.
VGIC recommends that the Commission revisit Rule 21 interconnection application fees
for small systems that are not enrolling in Net Energy Metering (NEM) or the Net Billing Tariff
(NBT). The previous Rule 21 proceeding (R.17-07-007) explicitly included this question in
scope but did not resolve it. VGIC recommends that the questions below from R.17-07-007 be
added to the scope of this new OIR under Topic 5: Tariff Implementation Costs or as a stand-
alone issue within the first phase of this proceeding:
• “Should the Commission revise the application fee for non-net energy metering systems?
How should the Commission determine what the revised fee should be?”
VGIC is concerned that the interconnection fee of $800 for all non-NEM and non-NBT
exporting systems are disproportionately high and not reflective of actual utility costs. Notably,
these fees significantly exceed those charged by utilities in other states, in most cases by 800%.1
Additionally, these fees have an outsized impact on bidirectional charging systems, which have
relatively lower upfront costs compared to stationary energy storage systems, rooftop solar PV,
or other DERs interconnecting under Rule 21. Since customers are purchasing EVs for
transportation uses, the incremental costs to unlock bidirectional capabilities is typically only the
cost of the bidirectional charger and interconnection. As a result, the $800 interconnection
application fee comprises one of the most significant upfront costs for bidirectional charging
systems and, in turn, a significant barrier to widespread bidirectional charging system
deployment. These deployments can support grid reliability, ratepayer affordability (including
1 See Attachment A for a list of national utility interconnection fees compiled by VGIC and members.
3

for non-EV customers), and efficiency through customer participation in a rate or program, such
as the Emergency Load Reduction Program, the California Energy Commission’s (“CEC”)
Demand Side Grid Support (“DSGS”) program, Pacific Gas and Electric’s (“PG&E”) Vehicle-
to-Everything (“V2X”) Residential or Commercial Pilots, PG&E’s Hourly Flex Pricing pilot,
Southern California Edison’s (“SCE”) expanded dynamic rate pilot, San Diego Gas & Electric’s
(“SDG&E”) Export Compensation Pilot, and/or SCE’s proposed Vehicle-to-Grid Resource
Proposal. These deployments can also help customers, including school bus fleets, municipal
fleets, and small business fleets, manage bills through non-exporting, grid-parallel bidirectional
charging system configurations designed to mitigate demand charges or energy consumption.
b. Interconnection Application Form and Portal Updates for Bidirectional EV
Charging Systems.
VGIC strongly recommends that the scope of this proceeding consider interconnection
application form and portal updates, specifically for bidirectional charging system Rule 21
applications, to ensure that customers can interconnect efficiently without confusion. VGIC
recommends that the following items be added to Topic 2: Interconnection Processes:
• What updates are needed to ensure each IOU’s interconnection application and portal
include clear interconnection guidance and process for bidirectional EV charging
systems?
To avoid confusion and delay, it is critical that each IOU’s interconnection application
forms and online portal, if available, allow applicants to clearly identify whether an energy storage
system is a bidirectional EV charging system. This update, detailed although ultimately not
directed in Decision (D.) 20-09-035 Issue 23f, is now more relevant as bidirectional EV charging
system interconnection requests have increased in the five years since the Working Group Three
4

report was submitted. Furthermore, applicants should be able to specify the technology type (e.g.,
AC or DC), as this can expedite utility review.
The IOUs have made some progress on this issue, but navigating all the IOU
interconnection portals continues to be confusing for bidirectional EV charging system applicants.
SCE and SDG&E do not have clear bidirectional charging system interconnection application
forms. PG&E does have dedicated forms, but customers aiming to be compensated for exports
under PG&E’s V2X Residential or Commercial Pilots must, confusingly, indicate that they are a
non-export application. The lack of appropriate forms has also led to confusion for utility
employees, with VGIC members reporting instances of bidirectional EV charging system
interconnection applications being routed out of the Rule 21 process to an energization make-ready
program or tariff (i.e., Rule 29/45). Consistent, standardized processes are essential to enable
bidirectional EV charging system project development and avoid unnecessary delays.
Lastly, per D.20-09-035, the IOUs must circulate quarterly interconnection timeline reports
showing the date by which certain interconnection milestones are reached for Rule 21 DERs. The
current quarterly reports are sortable by technology type. However, the three major IOUs use
different terminology for bidirectional charging systems and, in some cases, do not report the
bidirectional charging system requests, despite each IOU having known bidirectional charging
sites. VGIC recommends the Commission include standardized quarterly interconnection timeline
reporting for bidirectional charging systems in the scope of this proceeding under Topic 2:
Interconnection Processes.
c. Defining a timeline or roadmap for transitioning to virtual inspection and
notification-only approaches for non-NEM and non-NBT resources.
VGIC recommends that a new scoping topic be added on interconnection process
streamlining, which would include timelines for the development of virtual inspection and
5

notification-only approaches in Rule 21. Virtual inspections and notification-only approaches both
offer cost and time improvements over traditional site inspection processes, especially for smaller
bidirectional charging system projects. Establishing a clear timeline and milestones for this
transition will streamline interconnection processes and reduce burdens on applicants. For
example, the Commission could consider adopting a threshold number of bidirectional charging
site interconnections that would trigger the establishment of a virtual inspection process. VGIC
therefore recommends adding a new topic to the OIR on streamlining with the following questions:
• What milestones and timelines should the Commission adopt for implementing virtual
inspection and notification-only approaches for non-NEM and non-NBT projects?
What procedural processes should be established to ensure that these issues can be
taken up in a timely manner in the future?
• What technical or safety conditions should be considered when determine eligibility
for virtual inspection or notification-only interconnection?
• How can the Commission ensure that these processes evolve with market maturity?
Currently, all three utilities leverage virtual inspections to a certain extent to streamline
interconnection, particularly for NEM and NBT systems. However, field inspections are still
required typically for projects using relays, AC disconnects, NGOMs, and variances from Rule 21.
Meanwhile, the currently-approved notification-only pilot was available through September 2025
and was only open to non-export standalone storage or solar-plus-storage projects less than 30
kVA capacity.2
VGIC acknowledges that the bidirectional charging market may not currently be mature
enough to justify these approaches. However, we recommend that the scope of this proceeding
2 Resolution E-5311.
6

establish certain milestones or explicit timelines for when both issues should be taken up in the
initial Scoping Memo. VGIC advises against subjecting these issues to the organic cycles of
scoping new Rule 21 issues – as evident in Phase II of R.17-07-007, unexpected events can lead
to delays of 4-5 years, even for already scoped, time-sensitive matters. Instead, VGIC recommends
that this proceeding add a topic to consider what milestones (e.g., a certain number of deployments)
or timelines (e.g., a certain date for implementation) are appropriate to implement virtual
inspection and, subsequently, notification-only approaches. For example, the V2G AC pilot was
approved in D.20-09-035 and Resolution E-5315. In the Resolution, the Commission
acknowledged that it was premature to set permanent Rule 21 V2G AC standards but outlined a
concrete process for updating Rule 21 once V2G AC technical standards are in place, which will
involve the reconvening of a working group and the submission of a Tier 3 Advice Letter to update
Rule 21.3 VGIC envisions a similar process roadmap being outlined for virtual inspection and
notification-only streamlining, wherein a specific milestone, like number of sites deployed, is
adopted that would trigger subsequent Commission action.
d. Updating Rule 21 technical provisions for all storage devices and bidirectional EV
charging systems.
VGIC recommends that Topic 3: Interconnection Pathways and Standards add a question
to discuss any needed updates to Rule 21 to accommodate the wide variety of DERs now available
to customers, including bidirectional EV charging systems. With the large-scale and relatively
rapid adoption of solar in the state over the previous two decades, Rule 21 has adopted many
changes that are designed to serve that technology specifically. Therefore, some items in Rule 21
3 Resolution E-5315.
7

might not be fully applicable to less adopted technologies like bidirectional EV charging systems.
A more comprehensive review of requirements should be considered in scope for this proceeding.
Currently, the OIR contains a question on technical updates to Rule 21 for non-export
systems. This question also insinuates that these systems are creating “challenges”, which might
not be the case. Instead, VGIC recommends that this question be modified to address true technical
differences for both export and non-export resources:
• Do new technologies for non-exporting storage systems, including non-exporting vehicle-
to-grid (V2G) bidirectional EV charging systems, for electric vehicle charging stations and
home and building backup power, pose new challenges for interconnection introduce new
technical considerations or unique characteristics that merit consideration of changes to
Rule 21 for these technologies?
Revisiting these technical requirements to align with modern capabilities will allow DERs,
including bidirectional EV charging systems, to interconnect safely and efficiently.
B. ITEMS TO REMOVE FROM SCOPE.
a. Interconnecting customers should not be responsible for upgrade costs caused by
a sustained or permanent reduction in customer service loads.
VGIC strongly recommends removing questions related to the Responsibility for Upgrade
Costs Due to Load Reduction from this proceeding entirely. It is unclear why the Commission is
pursuing this issue in Rule 21 at this time, given that load reduction does not trigger significant
distribution upgrades and paying for load reduction is fundamentally antithetical to California’s
view of load reduction.
The OIR references Resolution E-5296 and the discussion on sustained load reduction. But
that Resolution noted that sustained load reduction is not a significant cost concern. The
Commissions states:
8

We further take note of the statement by IREC, that in response to a data request by IREC
to the Large IOUs, the Large IOUs indicated that they were not aware of even a single
incident in the last five years in which a distribution system violation occurred due to
sustained reduction in load. Given this response, we find that sustained reductions in
load, while certainly possible, remain somewhat speculative. We can’t say such load
reductions won’t happen, but are swayed by IREC’s argument that the likelihood
appears low, particularly given California’s electrification mandates.4
VGIC is unaware of any significant changes in circumstances that have occured since the
Commission adopted Resolution E-5296 in March 2024. In fact, the Commission notes that the
utilities should revisit the potential issue of sustained load reduction associated with systems
utilizing Limited Generation Profiles (“LGP”) three years after LGP options become available to
customers, which would be in mid-2028. 5 Without any data, it is premature and inappropriate to
adopt processes for determining the need for upgrades triggered by a sustained or permanent
reduction in customer loads, and even more premature to discuss the appropriate cost recovery
mechanisms. Given the expected load growth in the state, VGIC anticipates that upgrades due to
load reduction will be particularly significant. Limited stakeholder resources are therefore better
spent on the many other pressing topics in this proceeding.
More importantly, charging customers for reducing their electricity demand, whether
through energy efficiency measures, self-generation, or other means, would also be a significant
departure from the long-standing regulatory principles that promote customer choice and efficient
energy use. California’s energy policy framework has consistently encouraged energy efficiency
and load reduction as a public good, not a cost burden, because it advances decarbonization,
reduces system strain (including peak demand) and lowers overall capacity procurement costs.
Penalizing customers for not continuing to procure power from utilities at historic levels would
4 Resolution E-5296 at 34.
5 Resolution E-5296 at Finding 37.
9

effectively treat them as obligated “sinks” for utility sales rather than as customers paying for
services received. Moreover, customer load reductions occur for many reasons outside of the
installation of Rule 21 generators, including energy efficiency upgrades or pure operational
changes or business closures, and no customers are currently charged for grid upgrades that might
result from those decisions. If the Commission is concerned about the costs of load reduction, it
should be discussed in a venue where all types of load reduction can be considered and where cost
recovery can be discussed more broadly. This general discussion should not be in the scope of a
proceeding focused on Rule 21.
IV. PROCEDURAL SCHEDULE, CATEGORIZATION, AND NEED FOR
HEARINGS.
VGIC appreciates that the OIR recognizes the need for a comprehensive update to Rule 21.
However, given the breadth of topics under consideration and the Commission’s experience with
delayed resolution of critical interconnection issues, VGIC respectfully urges the Commission to
establish a procedural schedule structured around timely progress through parallel tracks and
targeted interim decisions.
The prior Rule 21 proceeding (R.17-07-007) addressed numerous technical and policy
issues in large, multi-topic decisions spanning hundreds of pages of discussion, resulting in long
delays and interdependencies between wholly unrelated subjects. For example, delays in finalizing
Limited Generation Profiles led to suspension of the entire proceeding in 2022, which has delayed
progress on urgent interconnection matters until the opening of this OIR in 2025. To avoid a repeat
of these challenges, the Commission should establish multiple concurrent tracks that allow
independent advancement of issues based on their complexity and urgency and, in turn, timely
conclusion via more manageable, targeted Commission decisions.
10

VGIC also strongly recommends that there be an early Track 1 dedicated to Interconnection
Application Fee Reform and Interconnection Process Improvements, as these constitute urgent
issues for bidirectional charging systems, which have not experienced the degree of stakeholder
focus historically afforded to rooftop solar and stationary energy storage. This track should focus
on near-term issues that can lead to tangible interconnection improvements and be resolved within
the next 9-12 months. These would include application fee reform for non-NEM systems and the
questions in Topic 2 on interconnection processes, particularly for interconnection application
form and portal updates.
VGIC supports the categorization of this proceeding and agrees with the preliminary
determination that hearings will not be necessary.
