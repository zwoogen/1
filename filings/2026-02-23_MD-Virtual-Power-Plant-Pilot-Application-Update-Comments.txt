I. Introduction
VGIC is a 501(c)(6) membership-based advocacy organization committed to advancing the role
of vehicle-grid integration (“VGI”) through policy development, education, and research. VGIC
supports the transition to decarbonized transportation and electric sectors by ensuring that the
value from flexible EV charging and discharging is recognized, compensated, and aligned with a
more reliable, affordable, and efficient electric grid.
VGIC’s comments focus specifically on the bidirectional charging system components of the
utilities’ revised Distribution System Support Services (“DSSS”) Pilot proposals. While the
pilots appropriately include bidirectional charging system participation, it is not currently
structured as the centerpiece of the programs. Given the DRIVE Act’s intent to position
Maryland as a leader in unlocking EVs as grid resources, VGIC’s recommendations are aimed at
ensuring that bidirectional charging moves from a modest pilot element to a scalable and

II. Comments
VGIC appreciates the opportunity to comment on the revised DSSS Pilot proposals submitted by
Baltimore Gas & Electric Company, Pepco, Delmarva Power, and Potomac Edison (collectively,
the “Utilities”).
VGIC supports approval of the revised Utilities’ proposals with targeted modifications. The
DRIVE Act is intended to position Maryland as a national leader in unlocking electric vehicles
as grid resources at scale. The Utilities’ revised filings represent meaningful progress toward that
goal. However, several refinements are critical to ensure that the pilots fully align with
legislative intent and do not inadvertently delay scalable bidirectional charging system
deployment.
1. Grid Parallel Operations with Export Should be Supported at Program
Launch
Grid parallel operations with export capability should not be deferred, as safety, reliability, and
certification frameworks have already been addressed through a robust stakeholder process and
subsequent Commission adoption of updated interconnection rules. Maryland has already
2

resolved the central technical, safety, and certification questions associated with bidirectional
charging system deployment. Through codified revisions to COMAR 20.50, the Commission
established comprehensive bidirectional charging system interconnection pathways covering
both direct current (“DC”) and alternating current (“AC”) architectures, including certification
options under UL 1741 SB for Vehicle-to-Grid DC (“V2G-DC”) systems and the emerging UL
1741 SC / SAE J3072 and UL 1741 SB CRD for V2G-AC systems. These rules provide clear,
industry-aligned standards for safe, grid-parallel operation of bidirectional charging systems,
including those exporting to the grid.
VGIC recognizes that Utilities are at different stages in deploying utility Distributed Energy
Resource Management Systems (“DERMS”) and Grid-Edge DERMS platforms. BGE is actively
implementing phased utility DERMS capabilities; Pepco and Delmarva outline a roadmap that
initially relies on a Grid-Edge DERMS vendor; and Potomac Edison proposes relying entirely on
a third-party edge-DERMS platform during the pilot. These differing deployment timelines
reflect legitimate IT modernization pathways.
However, these varying DERMS maturity levels should not be a barrier to allowing grid-parallel
operations with export from bidirectional charging systems at the outset of the pilots. Full Utility
DERMS deployment is not a prerequisite for safe, measured, and compensated exports.
Most notably, the Sunrun-Ford V2X Pilot has been operating for multiple seasons under current
architecture within the BGE service territory.
Meanwhile, in other jurisdictions, large-scale grid services have been unlocked using event-
based dispatch with existing metering and third-party platform integrations, rather than waiting
for fully mature utility DERMS. For example, California’s Emergency Load Reduction Program
(“ELRP”) pays customers for both load reductions and EV exports during grid emergencies
through day-ahead or real-time event notifications, relying on existing interval metering and
utility coordination rather than requiring a fully deployed DERMS platform. Another California
program, the Demand Side Grid Support Program: Option 3, directs aggregators to discharge
bidirectional charging systems when wholesale market prices reach a certain threshold.
Aggregators then submit device-level dispatch data to the program administrator to verify
performance during events. This passive dispatch approach enabled an incredibly rapid program
launch without relying on a DERMS provider. Meanwhile, California’s Self-Generation
Incentive Program uses a greenhouse gas emissions signal to coordinate battery charging and
discharging without a centralized DERMS platform.
Similarly, Massachusetts’ Connected Solutions program is a bring-your-own-device virtual
power plant that rewards customers for enrolling battery storage and V2X in aggregated grid
services. While utilities use DERMS-like platforms to manage device enrollment and dispatch,
3

Connected Solutions operates at scale, even though utilities have yet to fully integrated these
capabilities into their core operations systems.
Together, these programs demonstrate that export capability can be enabled early via simpler
operational mechanisms, and that resources can be brought into evolving DERMS and VPP
architectures over time, as the Utilities grow their own capabilities. Other jurisdictions have
demonstrated that bidirectional charging systems and distributed energy resources can be
unlocked effectively using these types of simpler programmatic structures during early market
phases.
Maryland can adopt a similar phased approach. During the pilot period, utilities can rely on a
mix of:
• Day-ahead event notifications,
• Aggregator coordination,
• Device-level measurement and verification,
• Standardized performance settlement processes, and
• Passive dispatch price triggers.
Therefore, VGIC strongly recommends the Utilities be directed to launch the DSSS pilots with
explicit support of grid-parallel operations and exports for approved interconnection applications
from the program’s inception. Additionally, bidirectional vehicles and chargers, operating as
load-only resources and/or backup-only resources, should be permitted to energize and operate in
load-only mode initially, with a seamless transition to grid-parallel with export once customers
enroll and satisfy existing interconnection requirements (including submitting an interconnection
application). This approach is consistent with the interconnection rules recently codified by the
Commission in COMAR 20.50. No additional procedural barriers beyond the codified
interconnection framework should be imposed.
2. Funding Should Scale if Market Adoption Exceeds Forecasts
The filings generally forecast modest participation of bidirectional charging resources during the
pilot term. However, the market is moving quickly. Bidirectional charging is transitioning from
limited product testing to fully commercial, mass-market offerings.
Major automakers, including Ford, General Motors (Chevrolet, Cadillac, and GMC), Nissan,
Kia, Volvo, and Polestar are already marketing grid-parallel and grid-supportive features. Tesla
recently launched its Powershare Grid Support offering in Texas, marking the first large-scale
vehicle-to-grid offering from a major automaker leveraging the lower-cost AC-based
architecture. Tesla has also detailed its plans to launch the same offering in California with all
three major investor-owned utilities. Additional announcements from Toyota, Honda/Acura,
Rivian, Lucid, BMW, Volkswagen, and Stellantis signal that bidirectional functionality will be
4

available across a near-universal suite of light-duty EV platforms within the next two to three
years.
On the charger side, dcbel, Wallbox, Ford, GM Energy, and Tesla already offer bidirectional
solutions, while Emporia, ChargePoint, Autel, SolarEdge, and Enphase have announced
forthcoming products. For example, Enphase has detailed its plans for volume production of its
IQ Bidirectional EV Charging Platform beginning in late 2026. These developments mean that
the bidirectional charging pilot program period will coincide with substantially broader
availability of bidirectional vehicles and charging equipment in Maryland.
The Commission should require utilities to:
• Clarify that funding may be reallocated across pilot pathways if participation exceeds
forecasts;
• Identify a streamlined mechanism for requesting incremental budget authorization for
cost-effective pilot expansion; and
• Avoid enrollment caps that constrain growth below market demand.
Maryland’s incentive framework should not become a bottleneck amid accelerating product
availability and consumer interest in bidirectional charging technology, as this would undermine
the DRIVE Act's clear vision and purpose.
3. Include Commercial and Fleet Bidirectional Charging Participation
All three Utilities appropriately allow commercial customers to participate in the DSSS pilots
through aggregated resource pathways. However, the proposals do not explicitly reference
commercial fleet bidirectional charging participation, despite the significant potential these
resources represent.
Among commercial segments, electric school buses represent the most mature and deployment-
ready bidirectional charging opportunity today. Hundreds of electric school buses are already
deployed nationwide, with many projects actively demonstrating grid export functionality.
School bus duty cycles, morning and afternoon routes with long mid-day and overnight dwell
times, align exceptionally well with distribution peak support and system capacity needs.
Additionally, school buses are centrally managed, predictably pre-scheduled, and located at
depots where interconnection can be strategically planned.
Importantly, the technology ecosystem supporting school bus bidirectional charging systems is
no longer experimental. Multiple OEMs offer electric school buses with bidirectional
functionality, and several commercially available DC high-power bidirectional chargers are
already deployed in pilot and commercial projects. Interoperability between buses and EVSE has
advanced significantly in recent years, with increasing use of the standardized communication
5

protocols for bidirectional power transfer outlined in ISO 15118-20. This makes school buses
one of the lowest-risk, highest-confidence segments for meaningful bidirectional charging export
during the pilot period.
Given this maturity, the DSSS program proposals should clarify how electric school buses and
other depot-based fleets (e.g., refuse) will be recruited. This may include coordination with
school districts, alignment with make-ready investments, and streamlined processes for enabling
grid-parallel export at fleet depots.
4. Structure Reporting and Stakeholder Engagement to Support
Transition to a Permanent Program
Quarterly reporting and regular stakeholder engagement is appropriate for pilots of this scale, but
these functions should serve a dual purpose: informing near-term program refinement and laying
out the transition to permanent programs. Each of the Utilities contemplates evaluating the pilot
before determining whether and how to transition to a longer-term offering. To ensure that this
transition is seamless, and not delayed by insufficient data, reporting requirements should be
explicitly structured to support program evaluation and stakeholder engagement from the outset.
Reports should go beyond enrollment totals and aggregate peak reductions. VGIC recommends
that each utility provide detailed data on export event frequency and duration, device-level
performance relative to nominated capacity (e.g., average, standard deviation), opt-out rates,
communications reliability, geographic distribution of participants relative to targeted feeders,
and other metrics that may support assessment of benefits and costs. These metrics will allow the
Commission to assess not only whether the pilots functioned operationally, but whether
bidirectional charging systems delivered measurable distribution and system value consistent
with the DRIVE Act’s intent. Transparent reporting will further clarify what refinements are
needed before transitioning to a permanent structure.
Importantly, the pilot-to-program pathway should not depend solely on a final report at the end
of the two-year term. Instead, the Utilities should use interim reporting milestones to identify
program design adjustments, necessary tariff modifications, and long-term funding requirements.
By the midpoint of the pilot, utilities should be directed to provide preliminary recommendations
for permanent program structure, including compensation mechanisms, eligibility criteria, and
operational protocols. If there is positive consensus during these periodic reviews, then pathways
to lock-in participation agreements beyond the pilot period should be made available to
customers. This forward-looking approach will reduce regulatory lag and provide greater
certainty to customers and aggregators considering participation.

Finally, VGIC encourages structured stakeholder check-ins at least quarterly to review
performance data and discuss program refinements. A transparent and iterative feedback process
will allow lessons learned during the pilot to be incorporated throughout the pilots rather than
deferred until the conclusion. These recommendations will allow Maryland to move from pilot
demonstration to a large-scale, cost-effective bidirectional charging program without creating a
participation cliff or sacrificing critical market momentum.
Conclusion
III.
Maryland holds a unique position nationally as the first state to codify comprehensive
bidirectional charging system interconnection rules covering both DC and AC architectures. That
regulatory certainty sends a powerful market signal to industry stakeholders that VGIC
represents. By accelerating export enablement, clarifying funding scalability, including fleet
participation, and requiring proactive pilot-to-program transition planning, the Commission can
ensure these pilots reflect the full ambition of the DRIVE Act.
VGIC respectfully urges the Commission to approve the revised DSSS Pilot proposals with the
modifications described above. With these targeted refinements, the pilots can unlock
meaningful peak reduction, accelerate bidirectional charging deployment, and position Maryland
as the leading state in realizing the transformative potential of electric vehicles as grid assets.
VGIC appreciates the Commission’s continued leadership and looks forward to ongoing
engagement on DRIVE Act implementation.
