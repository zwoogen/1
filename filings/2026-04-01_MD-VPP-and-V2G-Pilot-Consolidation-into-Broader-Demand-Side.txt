II. If Pursued, Align EmPOWER and DRIVE Act Programs
Through Functional Integration Without Delaying
Implementation
The EDCs propose closer alignment between EmPOWER and DRIVE Act programs, including
coordination of enrollment, administration, and incentives. VGIC does not oppose this direction
but is concerned that these efforts could further delay DRIVE Act implementation. The VGI
industry is eager and stands ready to advance programs that unlock EVs as flexible grid
resources.
If the Commission chooses to more closely align EmPOWER and DRIVE Act programs, VGIC
recommends functional integration, not structural integration. Functional integration means
aligning the parts of programs that improve outcomes, such as a common customer enrollment
experience, coordinated data and measurement, and interoperable communications and control
systems. Structural integration, by contrast, would merge programs into a single framework with
unified objectives, metrics, and incentive structures. EmPOWER is designed around energy
savings and curtailment-based demand response programs, while the DRIVE Act is focused on
dispatchable resources, with an emphasis on EVs as grid resources that can deliver time-sensitive
value such as peak reduction, capacity, and distribution system support. These differences lead to
distinct performance metrics, timelines, and program designs.
Attempting structural integration risks weakening both programs by forcing EVs and other
detachable resources into frameworks that are not designed to capture their flexible, time-
dependent value. This could constrain compensation, limit program flexibility, and reduce
overall grid benefits. A functionally integrated approach achieves coordination where it matters,
while preserving the distinct structures needed for each program to succeed.
III. Implement the DRIVE Act Consistent with Legislative Intent
by Treating EVs as Dispatchable Grid Resources to Reduce
System Costs
The DRIVE Act provides clear direction regarding the role of EVs in Maryland’s energy system.
The legislation establishes a framework in which distributed energy resources, including EVs,
are expected to support grid operations.
2

As defined in the Act, “electric distribution system support services” means “the dispatch and
control of a distributed energy resource to provide services that contribute to the efficient and
reliable operation of the electric distribution system,” including “local or system peak demand
reduction, demand response, [and] the avoidance or deferral of a transmission or distribution
upgrade or capacity expansion.”2 This definition indicates that the Legislature is focused on
resources that can be dispatched to meet system needs. The Act further reinforces this framework
by recognizing bidirectional EVs as resources capable of both receiving and discharging
electricity.
This language establishes that EVs should be treated as grid resources. If EVs are treated solely
as a controllable load, programs will focus on incremental peak reduction through managed
charging. If EVs are treated as grid resources, program design expands to include dispatchable
capacity, targeted peak reduction, and integration into virtual power plant structures. The
Commission should ensure that DRIVE Act programs are designed and evaluated in a
manner that reflects this intent.
Rising capacity costs in PJM create a need to bring additional flexible capacity onto the system
in a cost-effective manner. Research from New York3 and Massachusetts4 among others finds
that electric vehicles represent the largest source of flexible demand on the system, driven by
their scale, high availability, and multi-kilowatt capability. Compared to traditional curtailment-
based demand response resources, these characteristics allow EVs to deliver capacity more
efficiently and with lower administrative cost per kW.
In the near term, the most practical pathway to capture this value is the expansion of managed
charging. Managed charging is commercially available, has demonstrated customer acceptance,
and can be scaled across a large number of participants, providing a direct path to increased
participation and measurable peak reduction in the 2026 through 2028 timeframe.
At the same time, program design should explicitly enable bidirectional EVs at program
inception as VGIC recommended in our February 23rd comments in response to the EDC’s
revised DRIVE Act pilot proposals.5 This capability exists today and allows EVs to operate as

VI. Conclusion
VGIC appreciates the opportunity to provide comments in response to the EDCs’ filings
submitted pursuant to the Commission’s Notice of Public Hearing and Initiating Public
Conference 77. As the Commission considers the proposed alignment of EmPOWER and
DRIVE Act programs, it should focus on advancing coordination that improves participation and
performance without delaying implementation, while ensuring that DRIVE Act programs are
designed consistent with legislative intent. EVs, through both managed and bidirectional
charging, represent a practical and scalable pathway to deliver flexible capacity and support
system needs, delivering cost savings for all Maryland ratepayers. VGIC looks forward to
continued collaboration with the Commission and stakeholders to support effective
implementation of the DRIVE Act and the integration of EVs as grid resources.
