I. INTRODUCTION.
SDG&E’s Advice Letters propose to use Low Carbon Fuel Standard (“LCFS”) Holdback
Funds for two programs: a Residential Charging Rebate Program to provide incentives for Level
2 chargers1 and an EV High Power (“EV-HP”) Rate Incentive Program to encourage enrollment
in the EV-HP rate. 2 While these proposals support transportation electrification generally, they do
not include any vehicle-grid integration (“VGI”) component designed to actively manage EV load
or reduce peak demand.
This omission is significant. SDG&E’s own Electrification Impact Study (“EIS”) Part 2
demonstrates that managed charging and demand flexibility materially reduce peak load and lower
distribution infrastructure costs.3 Yet SDG&E does not currently offer active managed charging
or meaningful bidirectional charging programs, nor does it propose to use LCFS Holdback Funds
to develop them. As discussed below, VGI delivers measurable ratepayer benefits, and LCFS

II. SDG&E MUST INCLUDE VEHICLE-GRID INTEGRATION EFFORTS IN ITS
HOLDBACK PLAN.
A. Vehicle-Grid Integration (“VGI”) provides significant savings to ratepayers.
SDG&E’s Advice Letters propose to use LCFS Holdback Funds for a Residential Charging
Rebate Program and an EV-HP Incentive Program. While these programs may support
transportation electrification generally, they do not meaningfully advance VGI or actively manage
charging to reduce peak demand. This omission is particularly concerning given SDG&E’s own
recently filed analysis – its Final EIS Part 2 – demonstrating that load flexibility materially reduces
distribution infrastructure needs and ratepayer costs.
In its Final EIS Part 2, SDG&E found that maximizing demand flexibility led to a 193 MW
reduction in peak load and reduced distribution costs by approximately $697 million.5 SDG&E’s
analysis further confirms that light-duty electric vehicles (“EVs”) are the single largest
contributor to peak load growth.6 In other words, unmanaged EV charging materially drives
peak load and infrastructure expansion. Conversely, managed EV charging and broader
demand flexibility strategies meaningfully reduce those impacts.
However, these ratepayer savings depend on actually achieving the load shapes and
customer response modeled in the study. In its modeling, SDG&E specifically included active
managed charging strategies designed “to smooth charging demand during price fluctuations to
prevent rebound peaks.”7 There is also evidence that EV discharging using bidirectional charging
systems can further support load flexibility and drive down ratepayer costs. For example, Pacific
Gas and Electric (“PG&E”) found in its own EIS Part 2 that bidirectional EVs could provide over
30% of the load flexibility modeled in its Demand Flexibility scenario.8
Yet, despite these modeled benefits, SDG&E does not currently offer any active VGI
programs, including active managed charging or meaningful bidirectional EV participation
pathways. The absence of such programs stands in direct tension with the utility’s own findings

B. LCFS Holdback Funds Are Expressly Authorized to Support Vehicle-Grid
Integration Programs
As shown above, VGI provides measurable grid and ratepayer benefits, including peak
reduction and infrastructure cost deferral. LCFS Holdback Funds are not limited to passive EV
adoption incentives; they are expressly intended to support transportation electrification programs
that deliver grid and customer benefits. CARB’s updated LCFS regulation explicitly identifies
VGI-related investments, including managed charging and bidirectional charging deployment, as
eligible uses of holdback credit proceeds. CARB provides the following examples of permissible
uses: 9
i. Investments in grid-side distribution infrastructure necessary for EV charging.
ii. Support for vehicle-grid integration with projects such as:
I). Encouraging the optimization of EV charging through education in the following
areas: peak demand, rate pricing, grid emergencies, potential power shutoffs,
infrastructure deferral, renewable integration, and/or other signals and grid needs
to provide grid and customer benefits.
II). Providing program incentives to encourage driver participation in
monitored/managed charging, demand response, or vehicle-to-load / vehicle-to-grid
applications.
III). Supporting the deployment and installation of bidirectional charging equipment.
IV). Other innovative approaches to promoting and managing EV charging and
discharging that provides benefits to customers and the grid.

iii. Hardware and software that decrease the cost of or avoid updates to infrastructure,

These examples directly mirror the load flexibility strategies modeled in SDG&E’s EIS
Part 2. Managed charging incentives, bidirectional charging equipment deployment, and load
management software are not peripheral concepts; they are explicitly identified regulatory use
cases for LCFS Holdback proceeds.
Yet SDG&E is currently the only major investor-owned utility not proposing a VGI-
focused Holdback initiative. Given the documented cost savings associated with load flexibility
and the central role of EVs in driving peak growth, SDG&E’s omission is inconsistent with both
CARB’s LCFS framework and SDG&E’s own planning analysis.
Accordingly, SDG&E should revise Advice Letters 4794-E and 4795-E to include a
dedicated vehicle-grid integration component that:
1. Prioritizes active managed charging and bidirectional capability;
2. Explicitly targets peak load reduction and distribution infrastructure deferral; and
3. Aligns program design with the quantified load flexibility findings in SDG&E’s EIS Part
2.
LCFS Holdback Funds present a timely and appropriate mechanism to operationalize the cost-
saving benefits that SDG&E has already identified. SDG&E should modify the Advice Letters to
incorporate a VGI-focused program consistent with CARB’s authorization and SDG&E’s own
analysis. VGIC strongly recommends that SDG&E file supplemental Advice Letters
addressing this gap, proposing at least one VGI initiative.
